# Heterodimers and Oxytrex: Does the Ultra-Low-Dose Naltrexone Effect Have a Mechanistic Basis in Opioid Receptor Heterodimers?

*A cited investigation. Compiled June 2026.*

## Abstract / Bottom line

**The likelihood is low-to-moderate, and — critically — untested.** Opioid receptor
heterodimers (heteromers) are a *biologically plausible but entirely speculative*
explanation for the ultra-low-dose naltrexone (ULDN) effect exploited by Oxytrex
(oxycodone + 1–4 µg/day naltrexone). The phenomenon and the heterodimer literature
have developed on **parallel tracks that have never been joined**: every group that
actually studied Oxytrex/ULDN (Crain & Shen; Burns & Wang) attributed the effect to
**Gs/Gi G-protein switching** and the scaffolding protein **filamin A** — *not* to
heterodimers — while every group that built the µ–δ heterodimer story (Devi, Gomes,
Pintar) studied selective δ ligands and morphine, *never* ultra-low-dose naltrexone or
Oxytrex. No published study tests whether picomolar naltrexone acts at a µ–δ or µ–κ
heteromer. Compounding the uncertainty: Oxytrex's pivotal Phase III trial **failed**,
the flagship molecular-mechanism papers (filamin A) were **retracted in 2022** for data-
integrity problems tied to the Cassava Sciences/simufilam scandal, and the underlying
Crain–Shen "excitatory Gs-coupled receptor" model conflicts with mainstream opioid
pharmacology. The heterodimer hypothesis is therefore best described as an *appealing
analogy with no direct evidence and a difficult evidentiary backdrop*.

---

## 1. What Oxytrex is, and what actually happened to it

**Composition.** Oxytrex (Pain Therapeutics, Inc., later renamed Cassava Sciences) was
an oral immediate-release tablet combining the µ-agonist **oxycodone** (titrated
10–80 mg/day) with an **ultra-low dose of the antagonist naltrexone** — 1 µg per tablet,
i.e. 2 µg/day (twice-daily) or 4 µg/day (four-times-daily).¹ The rationale: at these
femtomolar-to-nanomolar tissue concentrations, naltrexone was claimed to *enhance*
oxycodone analgesia while *reducing* tolerance and physical dependence.

**Clinical record — one positive trial, one pivotal failure.**

- **Positive Phase II (osteoarthritis).** Chindalore et al. 2005 (*J Pain*) reported
  Oxytrex bid gave 39% pain reduction, superior to placebo (p<0.001) and to qid
  oxycodone (p=0.006).²
- **Positive Phase III (low back pain), 719 patients.** Webster et al. 2006 (*J Pain*)
  reported comparable analgesia with significantly less drug use and **~55% less
  physical dependence** (Short Opiate Withdrawal Scale) for Oxytrex bid vs oxycodone
  (p=0.01), plus less constipation, somnolence, and pruritus.³ Caveats: ~54% dropout,
  uneven baseline stratification, company-affiliated authorship.
- **Pivotal Phase III (osteoarthritis) FAILED.** Announced Nov 2005, it did **not** meet
  its primary endpoint: only a non-significant ~28% reduction in physical dependence vs
  oxycodone, undermined by 48–60% attrition in the drug arms.⁴ Because the FDA required
  *two* positive pivotal trials, the program effectively died; the company pivoted to
  Remoxy (which also failed at FDA) and then, as Cassava Sciences, to Alzheimer's.⁵

**Net:** the central claim (reduced dependence with equal analgesia) was shown in one
trial and **failed to replicate** in the confirmatory pivotal trial. The benefit of the
ULDN component was never independently established.

## 2. The ULDN phenomenon and its (non-heterodimer) proposed mechanisms

The observation Oxytrex was built on is real in animal models but mechanistically
contested, and **none** of the leading mechanisms invokes heterodimers.

### 2a. Crain & Shen — bimodal "excitatory" Gs-coupled receptors + GM1 ganglioside
In cultured dorsal root ganglion neurons, opioids shorten action-potential duration
("inhibitory," Gi/Go) at normal concentrations but *prolong* it ("excitatory") at fM–nM
concentrations; picomolar naloxone/naltrexone selectively blocks the excitatory mode.⁶
Extended in vivo: ultra-low-dose naltrexone (~10 ng/kg) enhances and prolongs morphine
antinociception and attenuates tolerance/dependence (Crain & Shen, *PNAS* 1995),⁷ and
blocks low-dose-morphine hyperalgesia to unmask analgesia (*Brain Res* 2001).⁸ The
proposed mechanism — opioid receptors coupling via **Gs** to produce excitatory
signaling, switched on by elevated **GM1 ganglioside**, and selectively blocked by
picomolar antagonist⁹ — is **largely a single-laboratory model** that conflicts with the
consensus that µ/δ/κ receptors are canonically Gi/Go-coupled. **No heterodimer claim.**

**Independent replication is strain-limited:** Terner et al. 2006 (*Pain*) reproduced the
effect in Sprague-Dawley and Long-Evans rats but **not** in F344 or Lewis rats —
i.e., the effect is genuine but not universal.¹⁰

### 2b. Wang & Burns — filamin A (FLNA) — *retracted*
The most concrete molecular account proposed that ULDN's true high-affinity target is the
scaffolding protein **filamin A**, which naloxone binds at a pentapeptide (FLNA²⁵⁶¹⁻²⁵⁶⁵,
"VAKGL") at ~picomolar affinity (~200× tighter than the µ-receptor), preventing a chronic-
opioid-induced Gi/o→Gs switch of the µ-receptor.¹¹ Preclinically this was applied directly
to Oxytrex: Largent-Milnes et al. 2008 (*J Pain*) reported oxycodone + ULDN attenuates
neuropathic pain and the associated **µ-opioid receptor–Gs coupling** — again, FLNA/Gs,
**no heteromer**.¹²

**Major caveat — the two foundational FLNA papers were RETRACTED in March 2022**
(*PLoS ONE* e1554 and e4282) for image-integrity problems (spliced Western blots,
duplicated panels, missing controls), as part of the broader Cassava Sciences/simufilam
research-integrity investigation. The corresponding author disputed the retractions.¹³
The FLNA mechanism — the field's "dominant" ULDN explanation — must therefore be treated
as unreliable. Note also the conflict of interest: Burns/Wang were tied to the Oxytrex
program and hold FLNA-binding analgesic patents.

### 2c. Glial TLR4 / (+)-naloxone — separate and contested
A parallel strand attributes some (+)-naloxone effects to blockade of glial **TLR4**
(Watkins, Hutchinson).¹⁴ This is a different dose range and the opioid-receptor-*inactive*
(+)-isomer; Skolnick et al. 2014 reported (+)-naloxone fails to inhibit TLR4 in vitro,
and the point remains unresolved.¹⁵ It does not explain the receptor-level picomolar
effect, and again involves no heterodimer.

## 3. The opioid heterodimer literature — strong "antagonist-enhances-agonist" phenomenology, but built on different ligands

Heterodimers are real and well-characterized in vitro, and one strand looks
*phenomenologically identical* to ULDN potentiation:

- **µ–δ (MOR–DOR) heterodimer and opiate synergy.** Gomes & Devi et al. 2000
  (*J Neurosci*) showed that **extremely low doses of δ-selective ligands increase µ-agonist
  binding**, and that a **δ-antagonist enhances both potency and efficacy of µ signaling** —
  i.e. the heteromer acts as a "brake" that a low-dose δ-antagonist releases.¹⁶ This is
  the same *shape* as ULDN potentiation, but the ligands used were δ-selective
  (naltriben/naltrindole), **not naloxone/naltrexone**, and the authors never framed it as
  the Oxytrex/ULDN mechanism.
- **δ-antagonism reduces morphine tolerance — the foundational in-vivo link.** Abdelhamid
  et al. 1991 showed the δ-antagonist **naltrindole** attenuates morphine tolerance;¹⁷ Zhu
  et al. 1999 (Pintar lab) showed **δ-receptor-knockout mice do not develop morphine
  tolerance**.¹⁸ The µ–δ-heteromer reviews interpret these as evidence the heteromer
  mediates tolerance (chronic morphine upregulates the complex; δ activation drives µ
  degradation; disrupting the interface prevents tolerance).¹⁹
- **But heteromers are not simply "potentiating."** The *same* µ–δ heteromer can be
  *anti-analgesic* depending on ligand (Milan-Lobo & Whistler 2013),²⁰ and the broader
  in-vivo physiological relevance of GPCR heteromers remains debated. µ–κ heteromers exist
  too, with sex-dependent analgesic roles.²¹

So the heterodimer field supplies a *mechanistically attractive template* — a low dose of
an antagonist relieving a heteromer-imposed brake on µ signaling — but it was constructed
with different tools and never pointed at ULDN.

**A further discount: the in-vivo standing of opioid heteromers is itself debated.** Much
heteromer evidence comes from BRET/FRET and co-IP in overexpressing heterologous systems,
which can report random "bystander" collisions rather than genuine complexes (James &
Davis 2006); the IUPHAR/NC-IUPHAR consensus accordingly requires a putative heteromer to
satisfy at least two of three stringent criteria — native co-localization, a heteromer-
specific functional fingerprint in native tissue, and loss of that fingerprint on
disruption (Pin et al. 2007).²⁴,²⁵ Massotte's 2015 review concluded that robust *in-vivo*
physical-proximity evidence exists for only a limited number of opioid heteromers and that
their physiopathological relevance is largely unestablished.²⁶ Single-molecule imaging
further shows class-A GPCR dimers are often transient (sub-second), and monomers can
activate G proteins on their own.²⁷ Thus, even before asking whether a heteromer mediates
ULDN, one must note that the heteromer *substrate* is on contested in-vivo footing — which
lowers, rather than raises, the prior on a heterodimer explanation.

## 4. Could heterodimers explain Oxytrex? Assessing the likelihood

**Arguments for plausibility (why the analogy is tempting):**
- The hallmark of ULDN — a *low* dose of an *antagonist* paradoxically *enhancing*
  µ-agonist signaling — is exactly the behavior Gomes/Devi documented for the µ–δ
  heteromer with low-dose δ-antagonists.¹⁶
- Naltrexone is not µ-exclusive; at higher occupancy it engages δ and κ. A heteromer with
  altered binding pockets could, in principle, present a high-affinity site occupied at
  ultra-low naltrexone concentrations.
- δ-antagonism robustly reduces morphine tolerance in vivo,¹⁷,¹⁸ which is precisely the
  clinical claim for Oxytrex.

**Arguments against / why the likelihood is not high:**
- **No direct evidence.** No study tests whether picomolar naltrexone binds or acts at a
  µ–δ or µ–κ heteromer, nor whether Oxytrex's effect survives genetic/pharmacological
  disruption of any heteromer. This is a genuine silence in the literature, not an
  oversight I failed to find.
- **The actual ULDN groups proposed competing molecular targets** (Gs-coupling, GM1,
  filamin A) that locate the action *off* the heteromer — on G-protein selection and a
  cytoskeletal scaffold.⁶⁻¹²
- **Dose mismatch.** The heteromer δ-antagonist effects (Gomes/Devi) operate in a
  low-nanomolar range; ULDN's defining feature is its *femtomolar–picomolar* potency and
  the loss of effect at slightly higher doses — a window the FLNA model was specifically
  invoked to explain and that simple heteromer occupancy does not obviously reproduce.
- **Oxycodone itself has no heteromer study.** Oxycodone engages µ, and contributes via δ
  (Yang et al. 2016) and possibly κ (Ross & Smith 1997, contested) as *monomers*; no work
  demonstrates oxycodone acting at a defined heteromer.²²,²³
- **Difficult evidentiary backdrop.** The pivotal trial failed,⁴ the FLNA mechanism papers
  were retracted,¹³ and the Crain–Shen model is not mainstream — so even the *phenomenon*
  Oxytrex relies on rests on shakier ground than a clean heteromer test would require.

**Verdict.** A µ–δ-heteromer basis for Oxytrex is a *reasonable, even elegant, hypothesis*
— it is the one alternative that naturally produces "low-dose antagonist enhances µ
analgesia and cuts tolerance" without invoking the contested Gs-switch or the retracted
FLNA data. But as of now it is **hypothesis-by-analogy with zero direct experimental
support**, and it must compete with (and has not displaced) the mechanisms the original
investigators actually proposed. Likelihood that heterodimers are *the* basis: **possible
but unproven and currently unfavored**; the honest status is *open question / untested*.

---

## 5. What a decisive experiment would look like

1. **Binding/BRET:** test whether ultra-low (fM–pM) naltrexone modulates µ-agonist binding
   or signaling *selectively in cells co-expressing µ+δ (or µ+κ)* vs µ alone.
2. **Genetic disruption:** ask whether Oxytrex's tolerance-sparing effect persists in
   δ-knockout mice or with a heteromer-interface-disrupting TAT peptide / bivalent-ligand
   control (e.g., MDAN-type tools).
3. **Isomer control:** compare (−)- vs (+)-naltrexone to separate receptor-level
   (heteromer/Gs/FLNA) from glial-TLR4 contributions.
4. **Independent FLNA replication:** given the retractions, re-test the filamin A binding
   claim independently before treating it as the competing mechanism.

Until something like (1)–(2) is done, the heterodimer explanation for Oxytrex remains
attractive but speculative.
